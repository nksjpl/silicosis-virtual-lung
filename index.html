Biological architecture

One disease process.
Multiple measurable layers.

Silicosis is a fibrotic lung disease caused by inhalation of respirable crystalline silica. At the biological level, retained silica can drive epithelial injury, macrophage-centered inflammation, matrix remodeling and, over time, a clinical disease phenotype. The interface below separates mechanistic plausibility from validated clinical use.

Current diagnostic anchor

Contemporary case ascertainment still depends on a credible occupational exposure history plus imaging and/or characteristic pathology. Biomarkers are investigational adjuncts; the proposed panel is intended as a triage or risk-estimation concept rather than a replacement for imaging, spirometry or occupational-medicine evaluation.

Biological scale
Scale 01 · Organ

Distal deposition concentrates injury in susceptible lung units

Respirable crystalline silica can reach terminal bronchioles and alveoli. Disease risk depends on cumulative exposure, intensity, duration, particle characteristics and context.

Context · exposed / no confirmed silicosis
Anatomical rendering is schematic. It emphasizes distal deposition and structural remodeling without implying that radiographic burden can be inferred from serum biomarkers.
Particles deposited in terminal respiratory units may persist and be engulfed by alveolar macrophages, creating a local injury environment that couples inflammation to epithelial and matrix responses.
The air–blood barrier is simplified but anatomically organized: Type I cells form the thin gas-exchange surface; Type II cells contribute surfactant and epithelial regeneration; macrophages reside in the airspace; fibroblasts occupy the interstitium; capillary blood lies beneath the barrier.
The panel rationale is cell-domain complementarity: epithelial injury/regeneration (KL-6), distal-airway epithelial integrity (CC16), and inflammatory activity (IL-8), with matrix-remodeling markers as secondary or exploratory signals.
Mechanistic arrows connect biological domains implicated in silica-related lung injury. They are not a validated causal model or diagnostic algorithm.
Serum accessibility makes KL-6, CC16 and IL-8 attractive for prospective evaluation, but none should be interpreted as a stand-alone silicosis test.

Translation boundary

Useful as a research signal.
Not yet a clinical verdict.

The most important design feature is epistemic: the interface clearly separates what is established in occupational medicine from what is promising in biomarker research.

The exact KL-6 + CC16 + IL-8 panel is not clinically validated across stages.

No statistically valid stage-specific accuracy or 95% confidence interval currently exists for this exact combination. Prospective multicenter validation is required before clinical or regulatory claims are made.

Current case ascertainment

Exposure history plus chest imaging and/or characteristic pathology remain central. OSHA silica medical surveillance specifies history, examination, baseline TB testing, spirometry and chest radiography; it does not specify a biomarker assay.

Recommended output language

A future validated tool should communicate a calibrated risk estimate for an exposed worker and trigger confirmatory occupational-medicine evaluation, not issue an autonomous positive / negative diagnosis.

Specificity challenge

Many candidate markers overlap with other ILDs, infection, autoimmune disease, COPD, renal dysfunction, smoking or systemic inflammation. Intended-use validation must include silica-exposed controls and relevant disease controls.
